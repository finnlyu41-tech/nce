# Independent blind review: NCE1 085–089

## Evidence and method

Read only `blind-prompts-085-089.json`, including its scope, contexts, and prompts. Solved all 54 tasks independently: 18 each for nce1-85, nce1-87, and nce1-89. The companion `blind-answers-085-089.json` contains the proposed answer, bounded alternatives, and item-specific issues. No lesson module, answer key, matcher, test, other course document, or author-agent output was consulted. No matching or key comparison was run.

Answers retain the specified subject, verb/object, location, time, and required adverb. Variants cover ordinary auxiliary contractions, alternative natural adverb placement, given time phrases placed first, and separable particles where applicable. Variant lists are intentionally bounded rather than exhaustive. All sentences have the requested terminal punctuation; case, whitespace, apostrophe glyph, or omission of a statement's final period are scope-level variations rather than separately repeated answers. Time phrases placed first can also naturally take a comma, though the supplied variants omit that optional punctuation.

`Has she yet heard the news?` and comparable medial-yet questions are grammatical but formal; the primary answers put yet last. `He's not ...` auxiliary contractions are especially familiar in British English; `He hasn't ...` is the more neutral primary contraction. Final still is natural with appropriate conversational emphasis but less neutral than `are still working` or `is still trying`.

## Findings

The prompts usually identify one clear target meaning. The strongest distinctions are completed result versus definite past in 85, unknown completion versus a known event's past timing in 87, and an unfinished result versus direct evidence of ongoing activity in 87. Course 89's duration questions make continuing residence/presence explicit and often supply a useful contrasting person whose stay has ended.

There is one recurring structural ambiguity worth deciding deliberately: unrestricted `live` prompts permit the present perfect progressive. `How long have you been living here?`, `I have been living here for five years`, `They have been living here since 2018`, `How long has he been living in London?`, and `He has been living here for ten years` are natural and preserve the specified facts. Those variants are included for the relevant items. The guided since-2018 hint's general `have/has + participle` wording does not expressly ban the progressive. If only `lived` should be accepted, the prompt should say so explicitly, as the two work questions already do with their `worked` requirement. This is an independent linguistic judgment, not a claim about any unseen acceptance logic.

Three localized wording concerns do not prevent solving:

- `guided-n85-completed-now`: the prompt mandates already, while the hint says to retain already **or just**. Keep already as the actual requirement; narrow the hint to avoid encouraging an answer that fails the lexical constraint.
- `independent-n85-definite-past`: an old-poster restoration report unexpectedly reports painting a bookcase. The English action is clear, but a furniture restoration report would make the scenario more coherent.
- `review-a-n85-definite-past`: a newly received past receipt for asking a question feels artificial. A meeting log or interview record would fit the event better.

`review-a-n89-not-completed-yet` says the readers have not finished the book. `They have not read this book yet` conventionally denies a completed reading and fits the mandated words, but does not explicitly express partial progress. That slight semantic coarseness is acceptable for a constrained elementary exercise. If partial reading is the key fact being assessed, explicitly permit or mandate `finished reading` instead.

The `repair` contexts ask to retain previous first answers and corrections but then clearly request only the new specified sentence. That workflow instruction does not supply linguistic evidence for this independent solve and should not cause extra response content.

## Bank novelty and transfer

The bank provides repeated practice across diagnostic, guided, independent, repair, next-day review, and week-later review. Most items still collapse to a small number of sentence templates once the mandated words are supplied. There are no named-person swaps, but pronoun/person/object substitutions often perform the same role.

- **85 completed-now:** six items vary subject, object, and participle, but all test the same `have/has + already/just + participle` decision. The contexts change current practical consequences, which helps meaning, yet this is mainly lexical and auxiliary practice.
- **85 visits:** the move from ever to never, from a named place to bare there, and especially the last just-returned question adds real distinctions. Paris versus London alone adds little structural novelty.
- **85 past:** verbs and time phrases vary, including a separable turn-on phrase. Every prompt explicitly says past and supplies a closed time. Useful irregular/lexical practice, limited independent tense selection.
- **87 completion:** six yes/no questions are largely the same frame. `Have you had breakfast yet?` adds the notable auxiliary/lexical have combination and several items add irregular participles.
- **87 follow-up:** six `When did ...` questions mostly vary person and verb/object. The repair prompt's speaker role is useful but the required you removes most referent uncertainty. Repetition reinforces did plus base verb; it does not create substantially new reasoning.
- **87 unfinished:** the alternation between uncompleted result and directly observed ongoing work/trying is meaningful transfer. `repair-n87-unfinished-state` and `review-b-n87-unfinished-state` are especially close: she versus they, trying to repair it, still, and nearly identical evidence. The latter is mostly an auxiliary/person swap.
- **89 duration question:** residence, employment, and presence give real predicate distinctions. Ended versus continuing stays in independent and review contexts add useful evidence. Still, mandated subjects and `How long` leave the actual sentence frame highly cued.
- **89 duration/start:** alternating a length with a calendar start adds a real for/since decision. Five versus ten years, or three months versus five years, are primarily fact substitutions; 2018 versus 2020 becomes structurally interesting only because live versus be also changes.
- **89 unfinished:** six items repeatedly use not plus perfect participle plus yet. Irregular done/spoken/gone/taken/read/seen offer useful morphological breadth; the completion logic remains the same.

Overall, there is meaningful distinction practice, especially at evidence boundaries, alongside considerable controlled-template repetition. These prompts can check constrained construction and morphology. Because most prompts directly name the intended tense or structure, success alone would provide weaker evidence of spontaneous tense choice in a less scaffolded setting.
